Positioning evmOS: customer complexity and community expectations

A retrospective on how customer conversations changed what we emphasised in the commercial offer — and why the existing Evmos community created a second positioning constraint.

Retrospective case study · evmOS

When you build a product, it makes sense to you because you already know why each component exists and how the pieces fit together. A prospective customer has to figure that out while deciding whether it is worth adopting.

I ran into this while working on evmOS positioning.

We led with EVM functionality and interoperability. Prospects asked about alternatives and whether our codebase was more complex. Those conversations pushed us to give integration and ongoing maintenance more prominence in the commercial offer.

My role

I worked on positioning with the CEO and CPO, drawing on competitive analysis and conversations with customers and prospects.

My work also covered the CEO’s pitch deck, website messaging and the launch campaign. I wrote the HAQQ and Realio partnership announcements linked below.

What the initial pitch missed

EVM functionality and interoperability were real capabilities, but buyers had other ways to build.

The alternatives we discussed included Frontier, an Ethereum compatibility layer for Substrate; revm, an EVM implementation written in Rust; and Polaris, a modular EVM framework with Cosmos SDK integration.

These were different starting points, with different capabilities and integration requirements.

EVM support alone did not explain why a team should choose evmOS.

When prospects questioned the complexity of our codebase, I understood that as a concern about the work adopting it would create for their team. Repeating the capability list would not answer that concern.

I would not treat every question about complexity as the same question. Some could come from unfamiliarity; some could reflect real engineering requirements.

What mattered was that customers were raising it themselves.

What we changed

We put more emphasis on integration help and ongoing maintenance, alongside bridging and customisation.

The team could help customers integrate the technology and keep it maintained. Customers could also extend their chains with their own Cosmos SDK modules alongside the EVM.

This gave us more concrete value to explain.

Buyers needed to assess what their own team would have to take on and where we could help. Integration and maintenance support deserved space in the pitch because they addressed that part of the decision.

The partnership announcements show how I communicated specific uses of the technology:

  • In the HAQQ announcement, published on May 23, 2024 , I explained how the evmOS upgrade gave Solidity contracts access to IBC. HAQQ’s own account described architectural limitations between its EVM and Cosmos layers that the integration addressed.
  • In the Realio announcement, published on May 30, 2024 , I explained the role of EVM compatibility and the flexibility to build custom Cosmos SDK modules, citing Realio’s recently launched multi-staking module.

These are examples of the product capabilities and my work explaining them.

They do not establish that the positioning change caused either partnership or reduced the teams’ maintenance workload.

The community constraint

There was another audience to consider: the existing Evmos blockchain community.

evmOS was the software framework behind the commercial offering, and the community already associated the project with openness and freedom - think of typical, most opinionated web3 audience. Keeping that community mattered, as the Evmos chain was still our main product.

A software licensing proposition risked clashing with those expectations. We had to consider how people would interpret the commercial offer and what they might think it meant for the project they already knew.

Explaining the value of integration and maintenance to a buyer did not automatically answer those community questions.

The chalenge of the positioning was that we had to account for both audiences - which had different concerns.

Customer conversations changed what we emphasised because they revealed something that was easy to overlook due to our familiarity with the product: buyers were assessing the work of adopting it as well as what it could do.
